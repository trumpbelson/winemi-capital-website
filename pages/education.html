Education

Structured learning for markets.

A progressive framework for reading price, understanding institutional behaviour, defining risk, and studying with reliable data — not noise.

Read in order the first time. Structure builds on structure. After that, return to individual sections when reviewing charts. The goal is literacy: recognising what the market has already done, where imbalance may still exist, and where risk is defined — not predicting the next tick.

1. Price is the primary language

Everything else is secondary. Volume, order flow, and news can add context, but price is the final expression of agreement between buyers and sellers. Learn to read it first.

2. Structure: highs, lows, and the path between them

Markets move in sequences of higher highs / higher lows (uptrend) or lower highs / lower lows (downtrend). Sideways periods are bases or ranges. Identify the dominant sequence on the time frame you are trading before looking for entries.

3. Supply and demand zones

Zones form where price left an area impulsively — leaving unfilled orders or a clear imbalance. Fresh zones (untouched or lightly tested) carry more weight than depleted ones. Location relative to higher-time-frame structure matters more than the zone itself.

4. The four structural patterns (RBR / RBD / DBR / DBD)

Rally–Base–Rally, Rally–Base–Drop, Drop–Base–Rally, Drop–Base–Drop. These describe how price arrives at a base and how it leaves. Understanding the pattern helps define the probability of continuation versus reversal at that location.

5. Institutional order flow concepts

Large participants accumulate and distribute over time. Liquidity is often taken before a move (stop hunts, sweeps). Absorption at key levels can precede a turn. These ideas are descriptive frameworks, not guarantees.

6. Multi-timeframe alignment

Higher-time-frame structure sets the bias. Lower-time-frame entries should align with that bias when possible. Trading only lower-time-frame noise without HTF location is a common source of inconsistency.

7. Risk definition before entry

Every setup needs a clear invalidation level and a defined risk size. Position size follows from the distance to invalidation and the maximum loss you accept. Without this, edge cannot be measured.

8. Complete setup checklist

HTF location → structural pattern → zone quality → confirmation / trigger → risk defined → reward potential. Missing any of these reduces the quality of the opportunity.

9. Common errors

  • Entering before confirmation
  • Moving invalidation after entry
  • Treating order-flow language as proof of a rescue
  • Ignoring higher-time-frame context
  • Over-sizing relative to the distance to invalidation

10. Process over prediction

The goal is a repeatable process that produces a positive expectancy over a large sample. Individual trades will lose. The process must survive them.

11. Journaling and review

Record setup type, location, risk, outcome, and emotional state. Review weekly. Patterns in the journal reveal what to keep and what to remove.

12. Psychology and capital preservation

Drawdowns are inevitable. Rules for maximum daily / weekly loss and for stepping away protect both capital and decision quality.

13. Systems and AI-assisted tools

Systematic rules and AI-assisted analysis can support scanning, pattern recognition, and journaling. Human oversight of risk limits and final decisions remains essential. Tools are assistants, not replacements for judgment.

14. Glossary

  • H / L / HH / LL — High, Low, Higher High, Lower Low
  • RBR / RBD / DBR / DBD — the four structural patterns
  • QML / MPL / DP — Quasimodo level, Maximum pain level, Decision point
  • FTR / FTB — Failure to return / First time back
  • SR flip / RS flip — Support–resistance role reversal
  • HTF / LTF — Higher / lower time frame
  • CP — Compression

15. Data sources for study and research

Sound education depends on reliable, widely available data. The materials on this site are structured for learning; they are not a substitute for primary sources. Practitioners and students typically draw on a combination of the following:

  • Charting & market data platforms — TradingView, MetaTrader, Thinkorswim, exchange terminals, and broker platforms for price, volume, order-book, and instrument history across equities, FX, commodities, indices, and crypto.
  • Exchange & official market data — NYSE, NASDAQ, CME, LSE, Eurex, SGX, and other venues for listed instruments, session calendars, settlement data, and official statistics.
  • Economic calendars — scheduled releases (CPI, employment, GDP, central bank decisions) from services such as Investing.com, Forex Factory, and trading platforms that aggregate consensus estimates.
  • Central banks & policy institutions — Federal Reserve, ECB, Bank of England, Bank of Japan, Bank of Canada, RBA, SNB, and related research notes for rates, liquidity, and macro context.
  • Statistical agencies — national statistics offices (BLS, BEA, ONS, Eurostat) and multilateral bodies (IMF, World Bank, OECD, BIS) for macro series and structural data.
  • Company & issuer filings — SEC EDGAR, Companies House, SEDAR+, annual reports, earnings releases, and regulatory filings for equity and credit analysis where relevant to study.
  • News & research wires — Reuters, Bloomberg, Financial Times, and similar outlets for event context (always cross-checked against primary data where decisions are involved).
  • Academic & open research — peer-reviewed papers, working papers, and open datasets for deeper study of market microstructure, behavioural finance, and risk.
  • Commitment of Traders (CoT) & positioning data — CFTC CoT reports and equivalent positioning data for futures and options to observe commercial vs. non-commercial positioning extremes.
  • Volatility & derivatives surfaces — VIX and related indices, options chains, and implied volatility surfaces for context on expected move and hedging demand.

When you practise on charts, prefer liquid instruments with transparent session times and sufficient history. Always treat third-party widgets and educational diagrams as illustrations — not as live advice or guaranteed data feeds.
